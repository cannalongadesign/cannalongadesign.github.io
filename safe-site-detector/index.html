Open the detector → 01 · Why it was hard

A trust product that had to be rebuilt without losing trust

Reclame AQUI is where Brazilian shoppers check a company’s reputation before buying, the closest local equivalent to Trustpilot. Its site detector tells people whether a store’s website looks safe. It ran on a partner’s engine, and when the partnership ended the partner would start charging about R$10 per query: R$1.7M a month at our volume.

Speed was one constraint. The other was the nature of the answer. A detector that labels a company’s site as unreliable is making a strong public statement about that company, and the old product had no measure of whether its answers helped anyone decide.

So I framed the work the way the business needed it, and held every decision below to it:

  • OutcomeRetire the partner’s detector before its per-query price turned into R$1.7M a month. That was the main pain, and the deadline.
  • Job to be done“Is this site a scam or not, before I pay?” The screen had one job, and everything on it had to serve that answer.
  • Who it had to work forDona Neide, an older woman in a small town in Acre, deep in Brazil’s interior, checking whether the store selling the skirt she wants is safe. If it was simple and clear for her, it was simple and clear for everyone.
  • OpportunityThe rebuild was forced by cost, so we used it to clear the debt the page carried: accessibility, a visual identity that did not follow the design system, and technical debt along the way.
02 · An experiment in six weeks

Live in about six weeks, measured from the start

To move fast, engineering built the first version AI-first: a crawler collects public signals about the site and the company behind it, and a model writes the analysis from four of them:

  • Site securitythe technical security of the site itself.
  • Phishing alertswhether the address appears in phishing alerts.
  • Companypublic information about who runs it.
  • Domaindomain data, such as how long it has existed.

Before launch, I ran a short design critique to cut cognitive load and scroll. The experiment then replaced the partner’s detector for about a month, with the decision metric Reclame AQUI uses across its products attached: a question inside the product that asks whether the information helped the person decide whether to buy. For the first time the detector had a success metric, and a baseline: a 45.1% decision rate.

More data, harder decisions

The experiment collected far more information than before, but it never said what it concluded. Every signal was left for the user to interpret, so a tool built to help people decide handed the decision back to them. The design problem became helping people understand the conclusion quickly, and why the system reached it.

03 · Decision: test comprehension
1

Test whether people read the result correctly

Problem

The easy question was “how do we lay this out?” A preference test would have picked the nicest screen and said nothing about whether people understood it.

Decision

I reframed the work around the user’s job on this screen and tested performance: does the person read the verdict correctly, and act in line with it? In Useberry, each person saw one version: the experiment, or a refined version I designed from the critique. Getting the verdict right, acting on it and effort decided the winner.

Why

A heatmap of the previous page showed attention almost entirely on the link field and the analyse button, and a benchmark of public tools like ScamAdviser and ScamDoc showed how others structure a safety result. Both showed where people look. Neither showed whether they understood what they saw, which is what a trust product depends on.

What it caught

Faced with a site that was safe, 46% of people using the experiment chose “be careful” and 25% “I don’t know”. Its extra data was making a safe site look doubtful.

Result

The refined version was read correctly by 74% of people, against 29% for the experiment, and it became the base for V2.

What the interviews added

People who finished the test could volunteer for an interview. The script never put screens side by side, so it measured the screen rather than my explanation of it. Four findings:

  • Reputation decides; the shields support it. The analysis matters most where a company has no reputation yet.
  • The verification seal didn’t explain itself. People described it through outside references, like a social network’s verified badge.
  • Trust and reputation only separated on a closer read. At first glance, a mixed result looked contradictory.
  • Shield colours confused people. They expected a traffic light.

Findings went back into critiques, and a MaxDiff ranked which questions about a company deserve space on the page. Each finding became a direction for V2: the verdict before the detail, the site being analysed at the top, a seal that explains itself, and traffic-light colours.

04 · Decision: a verdict built on evidence
2

A clear verdict, worded to state only what was verified

Problem

Shoppers need a clear answer, but a wrong claim about a company can harm a legitimate business and give it grounds to sue. Showing raw data avoids the claim and leaves all the interpretation to the user.

Decision

Every analysis ends in a verdict, from 0 to 5 shields, and each shield shows the signal behind it. The copy states as fact only what the analysis can prove: a site is called a scam only when we confirm it is stealing people’s data. Everything else is described as what we found, so the message lands without claiming more than the evidence supports.

Why

The shields make the verdict easy to read and easy to check: people see the conclusion and the reason for it in seconds, and the company can see exactly which signal shaped the result. Every word was reviewed with that legal weight in mind.

Result

V2 kept the depth of the analysis and gave people a verdict they can act on, with every statement on the screen backed by what was verified.

Five shields

  • Brand imitationwhether the address tries to pass as a known brand, or shows signs of data theft.
  • Site securitythe site’s certificate and technical structure.
  • Company identitywhether the company exists and its tax registration is active.
  • Consumer behaviourunrealistic offers, false urgency and suspicious contacts.
  • Reputation and sealthe company’s reputation on Reclame AQUI and its verification seal.

Six levels, one report each

Every analysis lands on one of six levels, from 0 to 5 shields, each with its own report: what was checked, which shield the site didn’t pass, and what to do next. While the analysis runs, the screen shows each source being checked. When a site can’t be reached, an error says so and offers a retry.

05 · Designing for AI readers

A second reader: the AI answering for the shopper

More and more people ask an AI assistant whether a store is trustworthy before they open any website. Engineering structured the detector’s data with JSON-LD, and I revised the interface to support it. Models favour question-and-answer content, so the page now opens with the question people actually ask, whether the site is trustworthy, and answers four short ones first: is it trustworthy, does the company exist, what is its reputation, does it have the seal.

Across 30 companies, citations in Google AI Overview rose 138%, measured in Search Console. The same screen now serves two readers: the person deciding on a purchase, and the assistant answering on their behalf.

06 · Impact

Cheaper, live, and easier to decide with

V2, the version that came out of the research, went live in stages: 20% of users, then 50%, then 100% in September 2026. At each step we watched for errors and user reports and adjusted before widening, so a wrong answer about a company would reach as few people as possible. It now runs for everyone on Reclame AQUI’s own engine.

Three things changed:

  • It answers the question people came with. The verdict comes first, and the shields explain it.
  • It is fair to the company being analysed. The verdict states only what the analysis can prove, and each shield shows the signal behind it.
  • It can be measured. The decision metric gives the team a running read on whether the detector helps people decide, which the old product never had.

The feedback loop is still running. Since reaching 100%, the back-end rules and the way the AI behaves have evolved considerably. When a corner case appears, we investigate whether the cause is ours or a configuration in that company’s own Reclame AQUI profile that is affecting the detector’s answer, and fix it at the right end.

How it was measured: the R$1.7M is the partner’s per-query price applied to our volume, not an invoice we paid; the R$1.2k is V2’s actual monthly cost for 171.7k analyses. Both decision rates come from the same in-product decision question, read during the experiment and after V2: a before-and-after, not a controlled test. The +138% compares Google AI Overview citations in Search Console for the 30 companies in the experiment.

07 · What I’d do differently

Hold the screen to its job, and align earlier

Most of what I would change is about focus and alignment, not the screen itself.

  • Hold every version to the job from day one. The crawler could gather far more than the screen needed, and even the refined versions kept wanting to show more. It took several design rounds to cut back to what answered “is this a scam?”. I would make the job the filter for every piece of information from the first sketch, to keep cognitive load low from the start.
  • Bring every decision level, and the neighbouring teams, in at the start. Decisions passed through several levels, and a direction approved at one could meet new context at the next and come back for rework. The detector also touches journeys other teams own, from B2C to B2B to the logged-in area. I would put the people who shape the decision together earlier, so context arrives before the design, not after it.
  • Ask the three-sided question first. Every decision has to be good for Reclame AQUI, for the company being analysed and for the consumer. If one of the three is left unprotected, the idea usually needs rethinking, and asking it at the start avoids rework later.

I would also look at the data companies send about themselves before designing the answer: a detector is only as good as what goes into it. It was my first time designing inside a market leader of this size, where many teams and decision levels shape one journey. Learning to work across those layers, and to adapt when the context changed, is what I take into the next project.

The team, and what was mine

A PM and the Head of Product owned scope and priorities, and engineering built the experiment, the analysis pipeline and the structured data. Mine: data discovery, the design critique, the research plan and comprehension test, the refined version, the shield structure and how each level is explained, and the interface changes for AI readers.
